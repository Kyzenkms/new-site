### 1.1 Number system & units of measurement
- India gave the world the **decimal place-value system**: value of a digit depends on its position (units, tens, hundreds…), with base 10. This system, with zero, is the ancestor of the modern number system used worldwide ("Hindu–Arabic numerals").
- The **Sulbasutras** (c. 800–200 BCE, part of Vedic literature) contain the earliest geometry and construction measurements in India.
- Ancient Indian units: length was measured in **angula** (finger-breadth); larger units included **hasta** (cubit, ~24 angulas), **dhanus**, **krosha** (~2–3 km), and **yojana** (~8 miles / ~12–15 km — the commonly cited value is **1 yojana ≈ 8 miles**).
- Time units: **ghati** (24 minutes), **pala/vighatika** (24 seconds), **kshana** (moment). Standardization of measurement was essential for Vedic rituals, astronomy, and architecture.

### 1.2 Concept of zero and its importance
- **Zero** (*śūnya*) as a **number** (not just a placeholder) is India's gift to mathematics.
- First explicit textual reference: the **Bakshali manuscript** (carbon-dated portions to the 3rd–7th century CE) contains a dot symbol used as a placeholder.
- **Brahmagupta** (628 CE, in *Brahmasphuta-siddhanta*) gave the **first rules for arithmetic with zero** as a number: e.g., a number minus itself is zero; zero plus/minus a number is the number; a number times zero is zero.
- **Importance:** without zero there is no place-value system; it makes large-number arithmetic, decimals, algebra, and ultimately computers possible. Aryabhata used place-value language ("sthānāt sthānaṃ daśaguṇaṃ" — "from place to place, ten times").

### 1.3 Large numbers and their representation
- Indian tradition had names for very large powers of 10, far beyond what Greek/Roman numerals could easily express — evidence appears in **Vedic texts, Jain literature, and Buddhist texts** (e.g., *Lalitavistara*).
- Commonly cited sequence: **1 (eka), 10 (daśa), 100 (śata), 1,000 (sahasra), 10,000 (ayuta), 1,00,000 (lakṣa/lakh), 1,00,00,000 (koṭi/crore), 1,00,00,00,000 (arbuda)** — and Jain/Buddhist texts go up to **10^62**-scale named numbers (e.g., in some lists up to *mahā-arbuda*, *asamkhyeya*).
- **Key point:** the decimal system + zero made such huge numbers writable and computable, enabling astronomy and combinatorics (Pingala's *Chandas-shastra* even contains binary-like patterns of long/short syllables).

### 1.5 Arithmetic operations
- The basic operations — addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, squares, square roots, cubes, cube roots — are systematically treated in **Aryabhatiya (Ganitapada)** and later **Lilavati**.
- Brahmagupta defined addition/subtraction/multiplication/division with positive and negative numbers and zero.
- Indian multiplication methods (e.g., **grid/lattice multiplication**, *kapāṭa-sandhi* "door-junction") and division algorithms travelled to the Arab world and then Europe.

### 1.6 Geometry
- **Sulbasutras** (esp. *Baudhayana Sulbasutra*, c. 800 BCE): contain an early statement of the **Pythagoras theorem** ("the diagonal of a rectangle produces both areas which the length and breadth produce separately") — centuries before Pythagoras; used for fire-altar (*vedi*) construction with exact proportions.
- **Brahmagupta's formula** for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral: √[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)].
- Bhaskara II gave proofs of the Pythagoras theorem and worked on areas/volumes.

### 1.7 Value of π
- Aryabhata (499 CE): **π ≈ 3.1416** — stated as "add 4 to 100, multiply by 8, add 62,000; this is the approximate circumference of a circle of diameter 20,000" → 62832/20000 = **3.1416**. He called it *āsanna* (approximate).
- Brahmagupta used **√10 ≈ 3.1623** (a practical approximation) and also 3.
- **Madhava (Kerala school)** computed π to **11+ decimal places (3.14159265359)** using infinite series — the most accurate value in the world at that time.

### 1.8 Trigonometry
- Indian origin: the **sine** concept — *jyā-ardha* (half-chord) → Arabic *jiba* → Latin *sinus* → "sine". So "sine" is etymologically an Indian word via Sanskrit!
- **Aryabhata** gave the first **sine table** (*jyā* table) in *Aryabhatiya*.
- Terms: *jyā* = sine, *kojyā/kojya* = cosine, *utkrama-jyā* = versed sine.
- Developed for **astronomy** (computing planetary positions, eclipses), later transmitted to the Islamic world and Europe.

### 1.9 Role of Sanskrit in Natural Language Processing (NLP)
- Sanskrit has an exceptionally **rule-based, formal grammar** — **Panini's *Ashtadhyayi*** (c. 4th century BCE): ~4,000 sutras describing Sanskrit grammar with algorithmic precision. Computer scientists call it one of the earliest formal systems.
- Features useful for NLP: highly **structured morphology** (roots + suffixes), **sandhi** rules (sound combination — like text normalization), **compounding** (*samasa*), unambiguous syntactic markers (case endings reduce word-order dependence).
- **Rick Briggs (NASA, 1985)** famously argued in "Knowledge Representation in Sanskrit and Artificial Intelligence" that Sanskrit's structure makes it suitable for knowledge representation in AI.
- Modern work: Sanskrit corpora and tools are used for **machine translation, speech processing, and semantic parsing** research; its formal grammar inspires parser design.

### Likely CAE-1 questions — Unit 1 (with model answers)

**Q1. Who gave the first rules for arithmetic operations with zero? State two of those rules.**
**A:** Brahmagupta, in the *Brahmasphuta-siddhanta* (628 CE). Rules: (i) a number minus itself equals zero; (ii) zero added to or subtracted from any number leaves the number unchanged; (iii) any number multiplied by zero is zero.

**Q2. State Aryabhata's contributions to mathematics and astronomy.**
**A:** Authored the *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE, at age 23). Gave π ≈ 3.1416; used the decimal place-value system; produced the first sine (*jyā*) table; explained that the Earth rotates causing day and night; explained eclipses as shadows (of Earth/Moon), rejecting the demon myth; solved linear indeterminate equations.

**Q3. What is the significance of zero in the number system?**
**A:** Zero as a number makes the place-value system possible — the same digit has different values by position. It enables representation of large numbers, decimals, negative-number arithmetic, algebra, and all modern computing. It was first treated as a number with operational rules by Brahmagupta.

**Q4. Which Indian text contains an early statement of the Pythagoras theorem? Who is credited and when?**
**A:** The *Baudhayana Sulbasutra* (c. 800 BCE) states that the diagonal of a rectangle produces the combined areas of its sides — centuries before Pythagoras (c. 570–495 BCE). It was used for laying out Vedic fire altars.

**Q5. How did the word "sine" originate from Sanskrit?**
**A:** Indian astronomers used *jyā-ardha* (half-chord), shortened to *jyā*. Transliterated into Arabic as *jiba/jayb*, then rendered in Latin as *sinus* (meaning "curve/bay"), which became English "sine".

**Q6. Name the Kerala-school mathematician who computed π to 11 decimal places and the method used.**
**A:** Madhava of Sangamagrama (c. 1340–1425 CE), using infinite series expansions for π (the Madhava–Leibniz series) — a precursor of calculus.

**Q7. Explain the relevance of Panini's Ashtadhyayi to NLP.**
**A:** Panini's *Ashtadhyayi* (c. 4th century BCE) is a complete, rule-based formal grammar of Sanskrit (~4,000 sutras). Its algorithmic treatment of morphology, sandhi, and compounding resembles modern formal language theory, making Sanskrit a model language for parsing, machine translation, and knowledge representation in AI (noted by NASA scientist Rick Briggs, 1985).

**Q8. Write a short note on ancient Indian units of measurement.**
**A:** Length: *angula* (finger-breadth), *hasta* (cubit ≈ 24 angulas), *krosha*, *yojana* (≈ 8 miles). Time: *ghati* (24 min), *pala* (24 s). Standardized units were needed for Vedic ritual geometry (Sulbasutras), astronomy, and architecture.

### 2.1 Indian astronomy — overview
- Among the oldest continuous astronomical traditions in the world; developed for **calendar-making, astrology (*jyotisha*), ritual timing, and navigation**.
- **Vedic period:** *Vedanga Jyotisha* (c. 1400–1200 BCE, attributed to Lagadha) — the earliest Indian astronomical text; 5-year *yuga* cycle; lunar mansions (**nakshatras**, 27/28 divisions of the ecliptic).
- **Classical period:** Aryabhata (499 CE), Varahamihira (*Pancha-siddhantika*, c. 575 CE — summarizes five schools), Brahmagupta, Bhaskara II — planetary models, eclipse theory, trigonometry-based computation.
- **Kerala school** (14th–16th c.): refined planetary models rivaling later European accuracy.
- Key insight to quote: Indian astronomers computed planetary positions with **epicycle-like models and sine tables** centuries before similar European work, and **Aryabhata proposed Earth's rotation** (~1,000 years before Copernicus's heliocentric model of 1543).

### 2.2 Celestial coordinate system
- The sky is imagined as a **celestial sphere** with the observer at the centre.
- Key reference points/circles (know the definitions):
  - **Celestial equator** — projection of Earth's equator onto the sky.
  - **Ecliptic** — the Sun's apparent annual path; the Moon and planets stay near it.
  - **Celestial poles** — projections of Earth's axis (north/south).
  - **Horizon, zenith** (point overhead), **nadir**.
  - **Meridian** — the great circle through zenith and the poles.
- Indian terms: the ecliptic is divided into **12 rashis** (zodiac signs, 30° each) and **27 nakshatras** (~13°20′ each); *kranti* = declination; *bhog* concepts relate to planetary longitude.
- Coordinates used: **longitude along the ecliptic** (measured from a fixed sidereal zero point — *Mesha* 0°) and **latitude** (north/south of ecliptic) — a **sidereal** system, unlike the tropical Western zodiac.

### 2.3 Elements of the Indian calendar
- The Indian calendar is **lunisolar**: months follow the Moon, years are kept in step with the Sun (solar year) via intercalation.
- Key elements:
  - **Tithi** — lunar day: 1/30th of a lunar month (time for the Moon to gain 12° on the Sun). There are 30 tithis (15 *shukla*/bright + 15 *krishna*/dark).
  - **Vara** — weekday (7-day week: named after planets — *Ravivara*, *Somavara*, …).
  - **Nakshatra** — the lunar mansion the Moon occupies (27/28).
  - **Yoga** — combined longitude of Sun and Moon (27 yogas).
  - **Karana** — half of a tithi (11 karanas).
- **Adhika-masa** (intercalary/leap month): an extra month added roughly every **32.5 months (~2.7 years)** to sync lunar months with the solar year; a month with **no sankranti** (solar transit) is counted as adhika-masa.
- **Saka era** (78 CE, starts with Kanishka's ascension) is the basis of India's **National Calendar** (adopted 1957); **Vikram Samvat** (57 BCE) is widely used in North India.

### 2.4 Traditional Pancanga (the Indian calendar system)
- **Pancanga** = *pancha* (five) + *anga* (limbs) — the almanac giving the **five limbs**:
  1. **Tithi** — lunar day
  2. **Vara** — weekday
  3. **Nakshatra** — lunar mansion
  4. **Yoga** — sun+moon combined longitude division
  5. **Karana** — half-tithi
- It also lists **sankranti** (Sun's entry into each rashi), eclipses, festivals, and **muhurta** (auspicious times).
- Regional variants: solar (*Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali*), lunisolar (*North Indian purnimanta/amanta*). Prepared by traditional almanac-makers using **siddhantic** (classical) or **drik** (modern observational) calculations.

### 2.5 Jantar Mantar
- **Jantar Mantar** = "instruments for calculation" (*yantra* = instrument, *mantra* = formula/calculation).
- Built by **Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II** of Jaipur (also founder of Jaipur city), **early 18th century** — five observatories: **Delhi (1724), Jaipur (1734), Ujjain, Mathura, Varanasi**. The **Jaipur** one is the largest and a **UNESCO World Heritage Site (2010)**.
- Purpose: correct the calendar/panchanga and predict eclipses and planetary positions with naked-eye masonry instruments of huge scale (large size = higher accuracy).
- Famous instruments:
  - **Samrat Yantra** — giant sundial; the Jaipur one is the **world's largest stone sundial** (27 m tall), accurate to ~2 seconds.
  - **Jai Prakash Yantra** — hemispherical bowl for checking other instruments' alignment.
  - **Ram Yantra** — measures altitude and azimuth of celestial bodies.
  - **Misra Yantra** — combines instruments; could indicate noon in multiple cities.
  - **Rashivalaya Yantras** — 12 instruments, one per zodiac sign.

### Likely CAE-1 questions — Unit 2 (with model answers)

**Q1. What are the five limbs (panca-anga) of the traditional Pancanga?**
**A:** (1) Tithi — lunar day (1/30 of a lunar month); (2) Vara — weekday; (3) Nakshatra — lunar mansion occupied by the Moon; (4) Yoga — one of 27 divisions of combined Sun–Moon longitude; (5) Karana — half of a tithi.

**Q2. Who built the Jantar Mantars, when, and name all five locations.**
**A:** Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II of Jaipur, early 18th century. Five observatories: Delhi (1724), Jaipur (1734), Ujjain, Mathura, and Varanasi. Jaipur's is the largest and a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2010).

**Q3. Explain adhika-masa. Why is it needed?**
**A:** A lunar year (12 lunar months ≈ 354 days) is ~11 days shorter than the solar year (~365.25 days). To keep festivals in their seasons, an extra (intercalary) month — adhika-masa — is added about every 32.5 months. A lunar month with no sankranti (solar transit into a new rashi) is designated adhika-masa.

**Q4. What is the Samrat Yantra?**
**A:** The giant sundial at Jantar Mantar observatories; the Jaipur Samrat Yantra (27 m high) is the world's largest stone sundial, telling local time accurate to about 2 seconds using the Sun's shadow.

**Q5. Define: tithi, nakshatra, ecliptic, zenith.**
**A:** Tithi — lunar day, the time taken for the Moon to gain 12° elongation over the Sun (1/30 of a lunar month). Nakshatra — one of 27 (or 28) lunar mansions dividing the ecliptic. Ecliptic — the Sun's apparent annual path across the celestial sphere. Zenith — the point on the celestial sphere directly overhead the observer.

**Q6. Distinguish the sidereal and tropical zodiacs used in Indian astronomy.**
**A:** Indian astronomy uses a sidereal zodiac: rashis are measured from a fixed star-based zero point (Mesha 0°). The Western tropical zodiac is measured from the moving vernal equinox. The difference between them — **ayanamsha** (~24° today) — grows due to precession of the equinoxes.

**Q7. Name the earliest Indian astronomical text and its main content.**
**A:** *Vedanga Jyotisha* (c. 1400–1200 BCE, attributed to Lagadha): gives a 5-year yuga cycle for tracking the Sun and Moon, lists nakshatras, and was used to fix the timing of Vedic rituals.

### 3.1 Ayurveda for life, health and well-being
- **Ayurveda** = *ayus* (life) + *veda* (knowledge) — "**the science/knowledge of life**". One of the world's oldest holistic medical systems, originating in India (Vedic roots; classical texts c. 600 BCE–200 CE).
- Foundational texts: ***Charaka Samhita*** (medicine/internal medicine — attributed to Charaka, based on Agnivesha) and ***Sushruta Samhita*** (surgery — Sushruta, "father of surgery," describes rhinoplasty, cataract surgery, 300+ procedures and 120+ instruments).
- Core principle: **health = balance** (*svasthya* — "established in the self"); disease = imbalance of doshas, dhatus, malas. Treatment is **personalized** to the individual's constitution (*prakriti*) and aims at **prevention** as much as cure.
- **Ashtanga Ayurveda** — the eight branches: kaya (internal medicine), bala (pediatrics), graha (psychiatry), urdhvanga (ENT/eye), shalya (surgery), damstra (toxicology), jara (geriatrics/rejuvenation), vrisha (aphrodisiac/fertility).

### 3.2 Understanding the human body and Pancha maha bhuta
- **Pancha maha bhuta** — the **five great elements**: **Akasha** (space/ether), **Vayu** (air), **Agni** (fire), **Jala** (water), **Prithvi** (earth). Everything in the universe, including the human body, is composed of these five in different proportions.
- Each **dosha** is a combination of two bhutas:
  - **Vata** = Akasha + Vayu (movement, circulation, nerve impulses)
  - **Pitta** = Agni + Jala (metabolism, digestion, body heat)
  - **Kapha** = Jala + Prithvi (structure, lubrication, immunity)
- The body is also described via **sapta dhatu** (seven tissues: rasa/plasma, rakta/blood, mamsa/muscle, meda/fat, asthi/bone, majja/marrow, shukra/reproductive) and **mala** (waste products: urine, stool, sweat).
- **Agni** (digestive/metabolic fire) is central: weak agni → *ama* (toxins/undigested residue) → disease.

### 3.3 Communication between body & mind
- Ayurveda treats body and mind as **inseparable** — mental states affect physiology and vice versa.
- The mind is influenced by the **triguna** (sattva, rajas, tamas — see 3.6), and mental imbalance manifests as physical disease (and physical imbalance disturbs the mind).
- Mechanisms of body–mind linkage in the tradition: **prana** (vital energy/breath) connects the two; **nadi** channels carry prana; practices like **pranayama** (breath control) and **dhyana** (meditation) regulate both.
- Modern parallel examiners accept: psychosomatic medicine — stress (mental) raising blood pressure or weakening immunity (physical) is the contemporary expression of this idea.

### 3.4 Health regimen for wellbeing — Dinacharya & Ritucharya
- **Dinacharya** — daily routine: waking in *brahma-muhurta* (early morning), oral/eye/nasal cleansing, oil massage (*abhyanga*), exercise (*vyayama*), bath, balanced meals at regular times, early sleep.
- **Ritucharya** — seasonal regimen: diet and lifestyle adjusted to the six Indian seasons (*ritus*: vasanta/spring, grishma/summer, varsha/monsoon, sharad/autumn, hemanta/pre-winter, shishira/winter) to keep doshas balanced (e.g., light warm food in monsoon when agni is weak).
- **Sadvritta** — code of good conduct (ethical/hygienic discipline): truthfulness, cleanliness, respect for elders — mental hygiene as health practice.
- **Ahara** (diet) is called *mahabhaisajya* — "the great medicine": eat according to prakriti, season, and digestive strength; avoid incompatible food combinations (*viruddha ahara*).

### 3.5 Introduction to yoga — raja yoga, ashtanga yoga, gyan (jnana) yoga
- **Yoga** = "union" (from *yuj*, to join) — union of individual consciousness with universal consciousness. Systematized by **Patanjali** in the ***Yoga Sutras*** (c. 200 BCE–200 CE): "yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ" — yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind.
- **Ashtanga Yoga** — Patanjali's **eight limbs** (memorize in order):
  1. **Yama** — ethical restraints (ahimsa, satya, asteya, brahmacharya, aparigraha)
  2. **Niyama** — observances (shaucha, santosha, tapas, svadhyaya, ishvara-pranidhana)
  3. **Asana** — posture
  4. **Pranayama** — breath control
  5. **Pratyahara** — withdrawal of senses
  6. **Dharana** — concentration
  7. **Dhyana** — meditation
  8. **Samadhi** — absorption/superconscious state
- **Raja Yoga** — "the royal path": the meditative path of Patanjali's eight limbs, focused on mind control and meditation; called royal because it directly disciplines the mind, the "king" of the senses.
- **Jnana (Gyan) Yoga** — the path of **knowledge/wisdom**: self-inquiry ("who am I?" — *atma-vichara*), discrimination between the real (*sat*) and unreal, study of Vedantic texts; suited to the philosophical temperament.
- (For completeness: the other classical paths are **Bhakti Yoga** (devotion) and **Karma Yoga** (selfless action) — from the *Bhagavad Gita*.)

### 3.6 Indian psychological concepts — consciousness, tridosha & triguna

**Consciousness:**
- Indian thought analyses consciousness in layers: the **four states** — **jagrat** (waking), **svapna** (dreaming), **sushupti** (deep sleep), and **turiya** ("the fourth" — pure witnessing consciousness beyond the other three), described in the *Mandukya Upanishad*.
- The **pancha-kosha** (five sheaths) model from the *Taittiriya Upanishad*: **annamaya** (food/physical), **pranamaya** (energy), **manomaya** (mind), **vijnanamaya** (wisdom), **anandamaya** (bliss) — layers from gross to subtle covering the atman.

**Tridosha** (three bodily humors — memorize the table):

| Dosha | Bhutas | Seat (main site) | Functions | Imbalance signs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vata** | Akasha + Vayu | colon (*pakvashaya*) | movement, circulation, breathing, nerve impulses, excretion | dryness, constipation, anxiety, insomnia, joint pain |
| **Pitta** | Agni + Jala | small intestine | digestion, metabolism, body temperature, vision, intellect | acidity, inflammation, anger, skin rashes, excessive heat |
| **Kapha** | Jala + Prithvi | chest/stomach | structure, lubrication, immunity, stability | heaviness, congestion, lethargy, weight gain, excessive sleep |

- **Prakriti** — one's inborn dosha constitution (e.g., vata-pitta); **vikriti** — current imbalance. Diagnosis and treatment are based on restoring the individual's prakriti balance.

**Triguna** (three qualities of mind/matter — from Samkhya philosophy):

| Guna | Nature | Mental traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Sattva** | purity, light, harmony | clarity, calmness, wisdom, compassion |
| **Rajas** | activity, passion, motion | ambition, restlessness, desire, agitation |
| **Tamas** | inertia, darkness, dullness | laziness, ignorance, confusion, depression |

- All three gunas exist in everyone in varying proportions; the goal is to cultivate **sattva** and transcend all three (*gunatita*).

### Likely CAE-1 questions — Unit 3 (with model answers)

**Q1. What does 'Ayurveda' mean? Name its two foundational texts and their focus.**
**A:** Ayurveda = *ayus* (life) + *veda* (knowledge), "the knowledge of life." The *Charaka Samhita* focuses on internal medicine (*kayachikitsa*); the *Sushruta Samhita* focuses on surgery — Sushruta is called the "father of surgery."

**Q2. Name the Pancha maha bhuta and show how they combine to form the tridosha.**
**A:** The five great elements: Akasha (space), Vayu (air), Agni (fire), Jala (water), Prithvi (earth). Combinations: Vata = Akasha + Vayu; Pitta = Agni + Jala; Kapha = Jala + Prithvi.

**Q3. List Patanjali's eight limbs of Ashtanga Yoga in order.**
**A:** Yama (restraints), Niyama (observances), Asana (posture), Pranayama (breath control), Pratyahara (sense withdrawal), Dharana (concentration), Dhyana (meditation), Samadhi (absorption).

**Q4. Distinguish Raja Yoga, Ashtanga Yoga, and Jnana Yoga.**
**A:** Ashtanga Yoga is Patanjali's eight-limbed system (ethical → physical → meditative disciplines). Raja Yoga ("royal path") is the meditative path emphasizing mind control — essentially the inner limbs of the same system. Jnana (Gyan) Yoga is the path of knowledge: self-inquiry and discrimination between the real and unreal.

**Q5. Explain the triguna with one example each.**
**A:** Sattva (purity/harmony — e.g., a calm, clear-minded student); Rajas (activity/passion — e.g., restless ambition and desire); Tamas (inertia/darkness — e.g., laziness and confusion). Everyone has all three; the aim is to strengthen sattva.

**Q6. What is the difference between prakriti and vikriti?**
**A:** Prakriti is the inborn, stable dosha constitution of an individual (e.g., vata-pitta). Vikriti is the current state of doshic imbalance. Ayurvedic treatment aims to return vikriti to prakriti.

**Q7. Write a short note on Dinacharya and Ritucharya.**
**A:** Dinacharya is the Ayurvedic daily regimen (early rising, cleansing, abhyanga oil massage, exercise, regular meals, timely sleep). Ritucharya is the seasonal regimen — adjusting diet and lifestyle across the six ritus (e.g., light warm food in varsha/monsoon when digestive agni is weak) to prevent dosha imbalance.

**Q8. Name the five koshas (pancha-kosha) from gross to subtle.**
**A:** Annamaya (physical/food sheath), Pranamaya (energy sheath), Manomaya (mind sheath), Vijnanamaya (wisdom sheath), Anandamaya (bliss sheath) — from the *Taittiriya Upanishad*.
